# G5.3 — Finite-sample theorem and proof

## 1. Definitions

Let \((Q_i,Y_i)_{i=1}^{N}\) be IID workflow-episode observations from a fixed target distribution \(\mathbb P\), where \(Q_i\in\mathbb R\) is a frozen predictive score and \(Y_i\in\{0,1\}\) is the trajectory-level bad-event indicator.

For fixed threshold \(t\), define:

\[
S_t=\mathbf 1\{Q\le t\},\quad \pi(t)=\Pr(S_t=1),
\]

and, for \(\pi(t)>0\):

\[
R(t)=\Pr(Y=1\mid S_t=1).
\]

Let \(\mathcal T=\{t_1<\cdots<t_K\}\) be fixed independently of the calibration sample. Define:

\[
n_k=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf 1\{Q_i\le t_k\},
\quad
m_k=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf 1\{Q_i\le t_k\}Y_i.
\]

## 2. Assumptions

**A1 — IID episodes.** Calibration episodes are IID from \(\mathbb P\), and the deployment episode population is governed by the same \(\mathbb P\).

**A2 — Frozen score.** The model, rollout procedure, score, outcome definition, and policy/tool version scope are fixed independently of calibration observations.

**A3 — Fixed threshold family.** The finite ordered threshold set \(\mathcal T\) is fixed before calibration, or is constructed from a disjoint pilot sample and then conditioned upon.

**A4 — Complete episode labels.** Every calibration episode receives a correctly evaluated binary outcome under the declared reference execution semantics.

These assumptions are not automatically true in production. In particular, A4 may require sandbox replay because formal enforcement censors actual prohibited effects.

## 3. Lemma — Conditional binomial law

For any fixed \(t\) with \(\pi(t)>0\), conditional on \(n(t)=n>0\):

\[
m(t)\mid n(t)=n
\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,R(t)).
\]

**Proof.** For each IID observation define three categories:

\[
C_i=
\begin{cases}
0,&Q_i>t,\\
1,&Q_i\le t,\ Y_i=0,\\
2,&Q_i\le t,\ Y_i=1.
\end{cases}
\]

Their probabilities are \(1-\pi(t)\), \(\pi(t)(1-R(t))\), and \(\pi(t)R(t)\). The category counts are multinomial. Conditional on the combined selected count \(n(t)=n\), the number in category 2 is binomial with success probability \(R(t)\). \(\square\)

## 4. Lemma — Clopper–Pearson upper coverage

For fixed \(t\), let \(U(t;\delta)\) be the one-sided Clopper–Pearson upper confidence bound:

\[
U(t;\delta)=
\begin{cases}
1,&n(t)=0\text{ or }m(t)=n(t),\\
\operatorname{BetaQuantile}
(1-\delta;m(t)+1,n(t)-m(t)),
&0\le m(t)<n(t).
\end{cases}
\]

Then:

\[
\Pr_{\mathcal D_{\mathrm{cal}}}
\{R(t)\le U(t;\delta)\}\ge1-\delta.
\]

**Proof.** Conditional on \(n(t)=n>0\), the preceding lemma gives a binomial count. The one-sided Clopper–Pearson construction is obtained by inversion of the exact binomial lower-tail test and has coverage at least \(1-\delta\) for every binomial parameter in \([0,1]\). Averaging the conditional coverage over the random value of \(n(t)\) preserves the inequality. When \(n(t)=0\), the upper bound is one, so coverage is trivial. \(\square\)

## 5. Fixed-sequence algorithm

Let \(\epsilon\in(0,1)\) be the target risk and \(\delta\in(0,1)\) the confidence error.

Initialize \(\hat k=0\). For \(k=1,\ldots,K\), compute \(U_k=U(t_k;\delta)\). If \(U_k\le\epsilon\), set \(\hat k=k\) and continue. Otherwise stop. Return \(\hat t=t_{\hat k}\) if \(\hat k>0\), otherwise NULL.

## 6. Theorem — Finite-sample workflow trajectory risk control

Under A1–A4, the fixed-sequence procedure satisfies:

\[
\boxed{
\Pr_{\mathcal D_{\mathrm{cal}}}
\left[
\hat t=\mathrm{NULL}
\ \lor\
R(\hat t)\le\epsilon
\right]\ge1-\delta.
}
\]

**Proof.**

If every candidate threshold is population-safe, the result is immediate.

Otherwise define the first unsafe candidate:

\[
k^\star=\min\{k:R(t_k)>\epsilon\}.
\]

This index is determined by the population distribution and the fixed threshold family; it is not selected from the calibration sample.

Suppose the procedure returns a non-NULL unsafe threshold. Then \(R(\hat t)>\epsilon\), which implies \(\hat k\ge k^\star\). By the fixed-sequence rule, every candidate up to \(\hat k\), including \(t_{k^\star}\), must have been certified. Hence:

\[
U(t_{k^\star};\delta)\le\epsilon
<R(t_{k^\star}).
\]

Therefore:

\[
\{\hat t\ne\mathrm{NULL},R(\hat t)>\epsilon\}
\subseteq
\{R(t_{k^\star})>U(t_{k^\star};\delta)\}.
\]

By the fixed-threshold Clopper–Pearson coverage lemma, the probability of the right-hand event is at most \(\delta\). Thus:

\[
\Pr(\hat t\ne\mathrm{NULL},R(\hat t)>\epsilon)\le\delta,
\]

which is equivalent to the stated result. \(\square\)

## 7. Interpretation

The theorem controls the probability, over the random calibration sample, that the selected non-NULL threshold has population trajectory-violation risk above \(\epsilon\), among future accepted episodes from the same target population.

It does not guarantee:
- \(\Pr(Y=1\mid X=x)\le\epsilon\) for every context \(x\);
- safety of every accepted workflow;
- validity under arbitrary distribution shift;
- validity when calibration episodes are dependent;
- production risk control from simulator labels without transfer assumptions;
- risk control after changing model, score, policy, tool versions, or outcome semantics.

## 8. Counterexamples to assumptions

- **A1 failure:** many episodes from the same tenant share latent state; treating them as independent inflates effective sample size.
- **A2 failure:** the JEPA model is retrained after calibration; the score/outcome joint distribution changes.
- **A3 failure:** thresholds are selected by searching calibration scores and then treated as fixed; the stated proof no longer applies.
- **A4 failure:** simulator labels omit a real-world side effect; the theorem controls simulator risk, not real-world risk.
- **Target mismatch:** calibration uses fixed action plans while deployment lets agents adapt their actions; the target distributions differ.

## 9. Existing-method novelty boundary

The fixed-sequence confidence-interval calibration principle is not claimed as new. See Dong, Hao, and Shinnou (2026), *Fixed-Sequence Confidence-Interval Calibration for Finite-Sample Selective Risk Control*, DOI 10.3390/stats9050109. UCON-JEPA's contribution must lie in the formal workflow-trajectory estimand, security event semantics, integration with usage control, and empirical evaluation against appropriate alternatives.
